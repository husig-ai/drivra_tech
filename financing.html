Competitive landscape

A local competitor is already running this exact model

RYD Nepal (rydnepal.com) rents Hero Super Splendor 125cc motorcycles to Kathmandu gig drivers today. Founded 2025, self-funded, 120+ bikes, claims 500+ active riders, single-SKU fleet. Only one of their three plans actually leads to ownership (Pro Monthly, Rs 7,000/week, after 1.5 years), which is worth noting since most of their marketing describes "own the bike in 18 months" as if it applies broadly. KYC is driving license + citizenship copy only, under 30 minutes, zero down payment, no app (web form + WhatsApp + in-person).

Category-wide finding: none of these companies, RYD Nepal included, have any real, independently documented UX: no screenshots, no coverage on UX pattern libraries. A well-designed, actually-screenshotted, transparent driver experience would be a genuine differentiator on its own, separate from pricing or terms.

The common thread across all three: wallet/account transaction history substitutes for a credit bureau score, and underwriting is handed off to a licensed bank/PSP partner behind the scenes, precisely the shape this product should take.

Recommended positioning

  • Lead differentiator: the only rent-to-own offer built inside an app the driver already uses daily for rewards/earnings tracking.
  • A compliant no-debt-spiral policy, not just messaging: M-KOPA's "missed payment extends the term, no penalty" policy is achievable as a compliant policy in Nepal too. NRB's hire-purchase rules cap penalty interest at 2pp/year and ban penalty-on-penalty compounding.
  • A visible ownership-progress tracker: percentage-owned, remaining installments, clear payoff date. No competitor researched currently shows this.
  • Tier-linked terms: better down payment or priority allocation for higher-tier Upahar drivers, a synergy RYD Nepal cannot replicate.

Nepal regulation

The single biggest thing shaping this design

Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB) regulation, not engineering choice, determines what's actually buildable. Under BAFIA, only a licensed BFI may originate or hold consumer loans, a pure tech company cannot lend directly (BAFIA 2073, Chapter 6, Section 31). Vehicle financing sits under a Hire Purchase Company license: NPR 300M minimum paid-up capital, a 60% LTV cap (at least 40% down payment), per the Sixth Amendment (roughly August 2026, only weeks before this research).

The single most load-bearing open legal question here: does a licensed hire-purchase company specifically get to invoke BAFIA §57's self-help repossession power? Sources conflict. This cannot be resolved by more research; it needs a Nepali banking/finance lawyer's direct read of Drivra's actual proposed corporate structure before the business treats fast repossession as a given.

Full regulatory detail: repossession, AML/KYC, CIB Nepal
  • CIB Nepal (the credit bureau) is only accessible to NRB-registered BFIs.
  • Repossession is two separate legal instruments working together, not one. BAFIA §57 is the substantive power: a licensed BFI can auction mortgaged/pledged collateral without a court order on default. The commonly-cited "35-day notice," though, is not written into §57 itself; it comes from a separate law, the Act on Recovery of Debts of Banks and Financial Institutions, 2058 (2002), read with Rule 4 of its 2059 Regulations. In practice they're used together (real published bank repossession notices consistently cite the 35-day figure), but they're legally distinct instruments, and the Debt Recovery Act explicitly doesn't apply below NPR 500,000 principal.
  • AML/KYC obligations apply regardless of structure: e-KYC with biometric verification under the Asset (Money) Laundering Prevention Act 2008; suspicious-transaction reports filed via goAML within 3 days; records retained a minimum of 5 years.
  • One documented exception worth knowing: NRB approved LTV up to 80% for businesses replacing vehicles damaged in the 2026 Gen-Z protests. Narrow and situational, not a general relief valve.
  • Also new in the Sixth Amendment: a cost-of-funds-based interest mechanism (replacing a flat spread cap), 10-fiscal-year license renewal, a higher PAN-requirement threshold, and a mandatory multi-brand financing requirement.

System architecture

A standalone service, plugged into the identity layer

Standalone service: own repo, own Postgres database, reusing Upahar's proven stack (FastAPI + SQLAlchemy 2.0 + Alembic + Jinja2/HTMX admin), but its own deployment and security perimeter, since it holds KYC documents and financial data. It reads driver identity and performance data from the canonical identity layer described in Architecture, never direct database access into Upahar or Express.

Auth

Driver auth: phone + SparrowSMS OTP, issuing a JWT scoped to a distinct financing_driver audience, deliberately not SSO-trusting Upahar's session for v1 to keep security boundaries independent. Admin auth mirrors the bcrypt+JWT pattern with more granular roles: KYC reviewer, underwriter, collections/ops, owner/compliance.

Storage: Supabase Storage over raw S3, for v1

At this scale (a few thousand drivers x about 5 documents), storage/egress cost is trivial either way. The deciding factor is integration effort: Supabase Storage wires directly into Postgres RLS. This must be its own Supabase project, never the one Upahar and GPS Dashboard already share.

eKYC: DIY Textract + Rekognition, for v1

Per-driver verification cost is roughly $0.07 (Textract AnalyzeID for 2 documents + Rekognition liveness + face-compare) versus $2 to $7 per verification for global vendors like Jumio/Onfido. DIY is roughly an order of magnitude cheaper; the trade-off is owning fraud-detection tuning in-house, acceptable when a human still signs off on every KYC approval regardless.

Three UI surfaces

Driver-facing web portal (not a second app), linked from Upahar. Admin console adding new tabs to the existing page+endpoint convention. A field-agent tool to record cash payments collected in person, matching how Nepali MFIs actually collect.

Credit scoring v1

Internal-only, rules-based

Every thin-file lender researched (Watu, Tugende) starts here: a rules-based scorecard, not an ML model, since there's no proprietary repayment history yet to train on.

Inputs available day one, all from Upahar: tenure, lifetime trips/gross and their consistency, current/longest streak, active status, tier/XP. Once the identity layer exists, also from Express: delivery tenure and reliability, for drivers who do both.

Cold-start handling: a guarantor requirement and/or higher down payment for drivers with little history, mirroring Watu's guarantor model and Tugende's reference-plus-training approach, rather than an outright decline. Decision flow: hard gates (KYC complete, active status, minimum tenure) then a weighted score, then approve / decline / refer-to-manual-review, every decision and override audit-logged.

Explicitly not in v1: telematics/GPS-based scoring or remote asset-disable enforcement. The Moove/M-KOPA/Watu pattern is powerful but adds IoT dependency and real reputational risk (documented tracker-collusion allegations against comparable East African lenders). Drivra's GPS Dashboard infrastructure already exists and could support this later, see Product, but it's a phase-3 decision, not a v1 one.

Payments & collections

No wallet in Nepal supports auto-debit: plan around that

Confirmed directly against each provider's own developer documentation: none of eSewa, Khalti, or Fonepay support true unattended auto-debit. eSewa's closest product still requires the customer to actively enter their token at eSewa on every transaction. Khalti's docs show no subscription/mandate capability at all. Fonepay's merchant API docs on this specifically weren't locatable, treat as unresolved rather than confirmed absent. ConnectIPS/NCHL supports mandate-based direct debit, already used for hire-purchase EMI collection, the best path to real automation for drivers with a bank account.

Default drivers toward ConnectIPS or Fonepay QR; support eSewa/Khalti as fallback, not the primary path. This v1 flow matches how the market leader (Hulas Finserv) already operates.

Compliance & risk

  • Every new table follows the Supabase PostgREST-grant-revoke migration convention.
  • KYC documents are the most sensitive data this system holds: Supabase Storage + RLS, encrypted at rest, 5-year minimum retention per AML rules, suspicious-activity reports via goAML within 3 days.
  • Vehicle lien registered both on the bluebook and at the Secured Transactions Registry Office under the Secured Transactions Act 2063; confirm the interplay of the two with counsel.
  • If Path A: CIB Nepal checks and §57 repossession privileges flow through the partner. The partnership contract needs to define who owns which compliance obligation.
  • Any future IoT/GPS/remote-disable capability needs a tamper-evident, auditable data trail from day one, given documented disputes in comparable markets over tracker reliability.
